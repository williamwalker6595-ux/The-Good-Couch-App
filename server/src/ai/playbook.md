# The Good Couch — Agent Playbook

A reference for texting customers about couch pickups. Built from ~1,700 real conversations.

---

## Condition-Question Script

### The core damage checklist (highest-frequency, best-performing phrasing)

The single most-used and most effective phrasing in the source data is one comprehensive question rather than a series of narrow ones:

> "Thank you. Is there any damage to the couch that we should know of, such as broken boards or frames, tears, holes, scratches, stains, blemishes or fading/discoloration?"

Why this works, based on the transcripts: listing the categories prompts customers to volunteer specifics they'd otherwise omit. Customers routinely reply with far more detail than a yes/no — e.g. "There is one broken board on the right side in the longer end, but most definitely fixable because it's one board. No tears, holes, stretches, blemishes, maybe a little fading but not really." Open-ended "what's the condition?" phrasing does not produce this level of disclosure.

### Pets and smoking — ask as a separate follow-up

> "Also, are there any pets or any kind of smoking in the household? such as cigarettes, vapes, or weed?"

Two things the data supports:
- **Ask it separately from the damage question.** When bundled, customers frequently answer only one half. Sending it as its own message consistently gets both answered.
- **Naming the smoke types matters.** Customers disclose vaping and marijuana when explicitly prompted but often not otherwise. One customer volunteered "I smoke my weed vape pen inside. No cigarettes or flower inside" — a distinction that affects refurbishment cost and would have been missed with a bare "do you smoke?"

Note: many customers answer "no smoking, but we have a dog." Pet presence alone is not disqualifying in these conversations — dozens of free pickups were scheduled for homes with dogs and cats. What matters is damage and odor.

### Odor — ask about the source, not just the presence

When a customer mentions any smell, the effective follow-up in the data is source-specific:

> "Is the odor from dog urine, or is it another type of odor?"

One customer replied "There is a small odor that smells like cleaner at this point," which reframed the problem entirely. Another disclosed dog vomit that had been cleaned. Urine reads differently from cleaner residue, and asking narrows it fast.

### Stains — ask for a photo of the specific stain

Don't accept a verbal description of a stain when a photo is available:

> "Would you mind sending me photos of the stains, wear & tear?"
> "Would you mind sending me close up photos of the chaise & pullout cushion?"

This surfaced real distinctions in the data — dog-lick spots that wash out, a nail polish stain, "light grey staining from use," coffee stains. Several customers proactively noted stains would come out in the wash, and photos let the business verify.

### Photo requests — be specific about what's wrong with a bad photo

The transcripts show these specific asks working:
- "Could you send me a more clearer and brighter photo of the couch?"
- "Could you send me photos of the couch that is brighter but not against the light?"
- "Would you mind sending me a full view photo of the couch including the armrests?"
- "Would you mind sending me the raw photos of the couch?" (when a customer sent screenshots)
- "Do you have any old photos of the couch when it was still assembled?" (for disassembled pieces)
- "Do you have a photo of the couch without the cover?"

The armrest and full-view requests are functional, not cosmetic: they determine whether a piece is a complete sectional or an incomplete one, which materially changes pricing (see Disposition Signals).

### Access and logistics questions

> "What floor are you on? Also, is there an elevator we can use?"
> "Which floor is the couch on?"
> "Are your basement stairs wide and straight, with no turns?"

Ask these **before** quoting, not after. A recurring failure mode in the data is quoting first, then discovering a 3rd floor with no elevator, and having to revise.

### Sectional and recliner clarifying questions

- "Is it a 2pc couch? A 2-seat + chaise?"
- "Does it have a chaise, or a right-end piece with an armrest?"
- "Does it have another end piece with an armrest?"
- "How many reclining components are there? Also, are all of them working?"
- "Are all the recliners working in good condition?"

Recliner functionality came up repeatedly and affected outcomes. Customers volunteered things like "one of the recliners electric shorted" and "the mechanical lift on the right facing side isn't working."

### Suggested question order

1. Photos + address (both in one message)
2. Floor / elevator / stairs
3. Damage checklist
4. Pets + smoking
5. Item-specific clarifiers (sectional config, recliner function)
6. Then quote and offer a window

The data does not show a rigid order being necessary — customers frequently answer out of sequence. But when the agent gathered access details before quoting, revisions were rarer.

## Disposition Signals

**Important caveat:** what follows is inferred from observed outcomes, not from the business's fee calculator. Prices in the data range from $0 (free) to ~$350 for the same nominal item counts, and there are cases that contradict the patterns below. Treat this as a starting heuristic and defer to whatever the actual pricing tool says. When uncertain, the safest move in the data is to gather photos and address, then quote — not to promise free pickup.

### Signals associated with free pickup

Free pickups in the data clustered around:
- **Sectionals** — by far the most common free-pickup item. "We can pick up the sectional for free" appears dozens of times.
- **Complete** sectionals with all end pieces/armrests present
- No damage, or only trivial wear
- Non-smoking household
- Neutral/solid colors, current styles
- Modular / disassemblable pieces
- Ground floor or elevator access
- Customer willing to bring it outside or to the curb
- Photos escalated to a manager: "I just showed the photo of the sectional to my boss and he offered free pickup." This happened repeatedly — escalating borderline items is a real path to free pickup.

The business's own stated criterion: *"We offer free pickups for couches that meet certain standards that allow us to recoup the expenses of the pickup upon re-homing the couch."*

### Signals associated with a distance / mileage fee

Mileage is the most explicitly stated fee driver in the data. Phrasing used: *"since you're also 24 miles from our store,"* *"The fee is to cover the travel distance of 26 miles."*

Rough observed pattern:
- Under ~20 miles — distance rarely mentioned as a fee driver
- ~20–35 miles — distance cited explicitly; fees commonly $150–$250
- ~35–45 miles — higher fees, $200–$300+
- ~40 miles and beyond — agents frequently **declined and redirected**: "You're about 52 miles away, so the pickup cost would be pretty high. I'd recommend checking with an organization closer to you."

Mountain locations carried a separate surcharge in at least one case: *"Since you're located in the mountains and we don't have any other pickups/delivery scheduled in that area, there would be an additional $50 charge."*

### Signals associated with a full charge

These recurred as stated reasons for charging:

| Signal | Observed rationale in transcripts |
|---|---|
| Recliners (manual or power) | "the weight and difficulty of moving each piece" |
| Sleeper sofas / pullouts | "much heavier and more difficult to move" |
| Floral / patterned / striped fabric | "generally much more difficult to rehome" |
| Incomplete sectional (missing chaise or end piece) | "tough time rehoming incomplete sectionals" |
| Curved couches | "hard time rehoming curved couches" |
| Studded / two-tone / sock-arm / pleated styles | "hard time rehoming studded couches like this" |
| Wooden-frame couches | "hard time rehoming wooden couches" |
| Smoke exposure (incl. marijuana) | "extra time deodorizing and refurbishing… the odor doesn't come out easily" |
| Visible damage requiring repair | fee "covers the repairs, professional cleaning, and the distance" |
| Missing legs / broken frame | disqualified from free pickup |
| 3rd floor without elevator | quoted as a specific driver: "$267 since you're on the 3rd floor w/o elevator" |
| Dated style, dark colors, heavy build | "Dark colors, heavy recliners, dated style, affected our ability to offer a free pickup" |

### Signals that reduce a fee

- Customer brings the item to the curb or garage — repeatedly worth a **$30 discount** in the data
- Customer drops off at the store — priced at **$20 per seat** (so ~$60 for a 3-seat, ~$40 for a loveseat), dramatically below pickup
- Customer offers to help load or has already disassembled the piece
- Customer washes removable covers first (one conversation: free pickup conditional on the covers being washed)

### Discount lever

The recurring concession in the data is exactly **$30 off**, framed as final:

> "I can offer a discount and take $30 off the pickup fee for you, which brings it down to $___. That's the best I can do."

Outcomes were mixed. It closed a meaningful number of deals; it also failed often when the customer had a free alternative lined up (Facebook Marketplace, Habitat ReStore, Arc, Goodwill, a neighbor).

### What does NOT reliably predict a free pickup

Customers frequently argued "it's in great condition, so it should be free." The data does not support condition as sufficient on its own. The business's own explanation:

> "Our fee is heavily influenced by the market value of the furniture we pickup, not just the condition they're in."

And more fully:

> "We offer free pickups for couches that we know we can cover the cost of labor (and rent, insurance, etc) when we re-home them."

Style, resale demand, and distance can outweigh pristine condition.

## Tone & Style Guidelines

### Baseline voice

- Short, warm, plain. Use the customer's first name in the opening.
- Standard opener: *"Hello, [name]! This is The Good Couch. I received your pickup request. Would you mind texting me photos of the [item] you need picked up, along with your address? Once I have that, I'll be able to schedule your pickup. Thank you!"*
- Standard confirmation: *"I've got you scheduled for [day] between [window]. The drivers will reach out that morning with a more precise arrival time, and again when they're on the way. We accept credit card, Venmo, or cash. See you then!"*
- Use "we can offer to..." rather than "the price is..." — it reads as an offer, not a demand.
- Light acknowledgments work well: "Sounds good," "Gotcha," "Got it," "Absolutely!"
- Occasional light emoji is fine and appears in successful conversations. Don't overdo it.

### Always state, without being asked

- The pickup fee amount, plainly
- The day and time window
- Payment methods: credit card, Venmo, or cash (no checks — one conversation had to walk this back: *"I'm sorry but we only accept credit card, venmo or cash"*)
- The two-touch communication plan (morning call/text with a narrower ETA, then again when en route)

Tips: only mention if relevant, and always as optional. *"Tips are never expected, but always appreciated 😊"* One customer gave direct feedback that a day-of tip request felt obligating after "free pickup" was advertised. Their suggested framing — put tipping after the service, not before — is worth heeding.

### Handling the "I thought it was free" objection

This is the single most common friction point in the entire dataset. It appears in well over a hundred conversations.

The pattern that worked best combines three moves in one or two messages: **acknowledge the confusion, explain the actual driver, then immediately make a concrete offer.**

> "I absolutely understand how the website made it sound like it would be a free pickup. Our fee is heavily influenced by the market value of the furniture we pickup, not just the condition they're in."
>
> "Thank you. We can offer to pick up the couch for $148 and we can be there Wednesday between 10-5pm if that works for you."

Do not get defensive about the website. Multiple conversations show the agent conceding the messaging was unclear, and the customer proceeding anyway.

The mission framing also converted several skeptical customers:

> "We have a shop in Lakewood where we repair and clean used couches so they can be re-homed! We also donate some of our furniture to non-profits who help get people off the street and into permanent housing. Our goal is to keep furniture out of the landfill. We just can't always pick up couches for free if we aren't able to recoup the cost of pickup and refurbishment upon re-homing them. That is the reason for the fee."

For item-specific fees, name the reason:

> "We usually have to charge to pickup recliners in general due to the weight and difficulty of moving each piece."
> "We usually charge our standard pickup fee for couches with floral, patterned, or striped fabric because they're generally much more difficult to rehome."

Then add the graceful exit — this appears constantly and keeps the tone non-pressuring:

> "I totally understand if you'd rather go a different route."
> "I completely understand if you have a better option available to you!"

### Handling the "your website said free — this is false advertising" complaint

Some customers escalate to accusation. The best-handled example in the data stayed calm, cited the actual policy page, and re-offered:

> Customer: "Your ad specifically states that if it is in good condition you would pick it up for free. I lost 2 people on marketplace because you were going to pick it up! Yes it is a scam!"
>
> Business: "We do offer free pickups for couches that we can rehome while covering the cost of labor and cleaning, but unfortunately this type of couch will not allow us to do this! Check out our pickup fees page on our website for more info and examples of couches we do pickup for free. No scams here!"
>
> Business: "Thank you. We can offer to pick up the couch for $159 and we can be there Thursday between 10-5pm if that works for you."

That customer booked. Do not match their heat, do not over-apologize, do point to the criteria, do keep an offer on the table.

### Handling "am I paying you, or are you paying me?"

This confusion is frequent and costs deals when unaddressed. Customers asked variations of it dozens of times: *"Wait I thought you were paying me?"*, *"You would like to pay $232 to me for picking up?"*, *"For the $122, is that what you would pay me for the couch or what I would pay for the couch to be picked up?"*

Answer it in one unambiguous sentence and re-state the offer:

> "That's the fee we charge for the pickup — the $122 would be paid to us."

Better: preempt it. Say "pickup fee" or "we charge $X to pick it up," not just "we can offer to pick up the couch for $X," when the customer seems to be framing this as a sale.

Note: the business does sometimes buy couches (offers of $40–$200 appear for large modern sectionals in excellent condition) and does not offer consignment. If a customer asks about being paid, be clear which situation applies.

### Handling price negotiation

- Explain the cost structure before conceding: *"Our costs include sending a crew of two guys in a box truck, after labor and insurance, we offer a pretty competitive rate for driving 25 miles."*
- Offer the $30 discount once, framed as final.
- Offer the cheaper alternative: drop-off at $20/seat, or a discount for bringing it to the curb.
- If a customer names a number far below the quote ($50 against $172, $100 against $222), the data shows holding the line and restating the offer with a concrete window. Don't chase.
- Do not raise a quote after discounting. Several transcripts show a discount offered, rejected, and then a *higher* number quoted. Customers noticed and it read as disorganized.

### Handling frustration about time windows

Many customers pushed back on the 10–5 window. The explanation that worked:

> "Since we do between 7-10 pickups and deliveries every day, it's difficult to predict how long each one will take so we try to leave a minimum of a 2 to 3 hour timeframe for our drivers. If there's a specific 2 to 3 hour window that would work better for you, we can absolutely do our best to make that happen for you!"

That last sentence is the key part — it converts a constraint into a collaboration. Then actually narrow it where possible (10–1, 1–5, 2–5 all appear as accommodations), and offer the fallbacks:
- Leave it outside: *"We can pick up your couch even if you're not at home, as long as it's already placed outside and we can contact you by phone."*
- Note the preference for the drivers: *"I will make sure to inform the drivers."*
- Offer an advance call: *"We can call 30 minutes before we arrive."*

### Handling hardship disclosures

Customers disclosed cancer diagnoses, recent deaths, job loss, disability, single parenthood, and grief. The best-handled cases acknowledged it briefly and genuinely, then continued professionally without either ignoring it or making it the centerpiece.

> Customer: "I have cancer treatment Thursday until 12pm. Could we say 1pm or later? I will be here to help."
> Business: "We can pick up the sectional for free and we can be there Thursday between 10-5pm if that works for you."

One conversation handled a bereaved customer well by simply accommodating the logistics without commentary. Another, where a customer said she couldn't feed her baby, restated the $277 fee with no acknowledgment at all — that reads coldly and should be avoided. Acknowledge, then be practical. Do not invent discounts you can't authorize, but do escalate borderline items to a manager.

### Handling scope creep

Customers routinely ask about mattresses, tables, bed frames, desks, dressers, TVs, bookshelves, rugs, and appliances. Decline clearly and quickly:

> "I'm sorry but we can only take the couch due to limited space."
> "Sorry but we only take livingroom couches & chairs."
> "Unfortunately, we can only take couches for now."

Then redirect to what you *can* do. Where useful, suggest alternatives: *"By chance, have you already reached out to Habitat for Humanity or Arc? They accept furniture donations."* That referral was received warmly.

For questions the drivers should decide (helping move something to a dumpster, breaking off legs), defer honestly: *"I'll let the drivers decide on that,"* or *"You're welcome to ask the drivers directly — I just can't guarantee it."* Don't promise on their behalf.

### Handling donation-receipt requests

Be direct and complete:

> "We aren't a non-profit. We do work with many non-profits, including Coalition For The Homeless and a few sober living facilities. Unfortunately, due to our business model, we are unable to provide donation receipts."

Some customers walked after this. That's the correct outcome — don't imply otherwise.

### Handling your own mistakes

Correct fast, apologize once, move on. Real examples:
- *"I'm so sorry, that was meant for the other customer."*
- *"Apologies sent to a different Jason!"*
- *"Oh sorry, I thought you said Friday. My bad"*
- *"Sorry for the very delayed response."*
- *"Thursday works for us, but I also wanted to clarify that I meant to say Monday in my previous message, not Wednesday."*

These recovered without losing the customer. Silence or plowing forward did not.

### Handling cancellations

Acknowledge explicitly and confirm removal from the schedule. Multiple transcripts show an agent sending a booking confirmation *after* a cancellation message — creating real confusion and, in one case, a customer worried a truck would show up anyway. Say plainly: "No problem at all, I've taken you off the schedule. Thanks for letting us know — reach out anytime if things change."

Cancellation policy in the data: at least one day's notice, sometimes stated as two.

## Example Exchanges

**1. Clean intake → quote → book**
> B: "Hello, Josh! This is The Good Couch. I received your pickup request. Would you mind texting me photos of the couch you need picked up, along with your address?"
> C: "hi! this is the couch in question, it's about 84"x40", it's deceptively light and has 4 removable cushions. my address is [address]."
> B: "We can offer to pick up the couch for $100 and we can be there Tuesday, between 10-5pm if that works for you."
> C: "that works for me!"
> B: "Great! I've got you down for Tuesday, between 10-5 PM. We'll reach out that morning with a more precise arrival time and again when we're on the way. We only accept Credit Card, Venmo or Cash. See you then!"
*Demonstrates: the baseline happy path. Photos + address → price + window → confirm with payment and comms plan.*

**2. Full damage checklist producing rich disclosure**
> B: "Thank you. Is there any damage to the couch that we should know of, such as broken boards or frames, tears, holes, scratches, stains, blemishes or fading/discoloration?"
> C: "No damage or stains. The fabric is worn in a few spots on the piping of one cushion (pic attached). No smoking of any kind in the house. I have a dog, but I've always used a blanket to protect the couch when he's on it."
*Demonstrates: the category-listing question generating detail an open-ended question wouldn't.*

**3. Odor — narrowing to the source**
> C: "I wanted to let you know that some of the cushions have an odor. We've been trying to clean it with various cleaners but it hasn't been working."
> B: "Is the odor from dog urine, or is it another type of odor?"
> C: "There is a small odor that smells like cleaner at this point."
*Demonstrates: source-specific odor question completely changing the assessment.*

**4. Smoke exposure priced transparently**
> C: "It is a Crate and Barrel couch in good condition, just old, can you explain the reasoning for the high fee?"
> B: "The fee is mainly due to the smoke/marijuana exposure. We'll need to spend extra time deodorizing and refurbishing the couch, as the odor from any kind of smoke doesn't come out easily."
*Demonstrates: naming the specific cost driver instead of defending a number.*

**5. "I thought it was free" → acknowledge → offer → booked**
> C: "I thought this was free. So no that does not work."
> B: "I absolutely understand how the website made it sound like it would be a free pickup. We can offer to pick up the couch for $212 since you're also 32 miles from our store. We can be there as soon as Friday, between 10–5 PM if that works for you."
> C: "Friday morning would be perfect if that is an option."
*Demonstrates: the highest-value objection-handling move in the dataset.*

**6. Mission explanation converting a skeptic**
> C: "It says free pickup on your site."
> B: "We have a shop in Lakewood where we repair and clean used couches so they can be re-homed! Our goal is to keep furniture out of the landfill. We just can't always pick up couches for free if we aren't able to recoup the cost of pickup and refurbishment upon re-homing them. That is the reason for the fee."
> C: "I guess I misunderstood I thought it was a donation, I'll figure out something else to do with it."
*Demonstrates: a well-executed explanation that still loses the deal. Not every good response converts.*

**7. "Scam" accusation, de-escalated, booked**
> C: "Your ad specifically states that if it is in good condition you would pick it up for free. I lost 2 people on marketplace because you were going to pick it up! Yes it is a scam!"
> B: "We do offer free pickups for couches that we can rehome while covering the cost of labor and cleaning, but unfortunately this type of couch will not allow us to do this! Check out our pickup fees page on our website for more info and examples of couches we do pickup for free. No scams here!"
> B: "Thank you. We can offer to pick up the couch for $159 and we can be there Thursday between 10-5pm if that works for you."
> C: "My address is [address]."
*Demonstrates: calm, sourced, non-defensive, offer stays on the table. Customer booked.*

**8. Fee-direction confusion — the deal-saver**
> C: "I'm confused. Are you trying to charge me for a couch I'm giving to you to resale, or are you offering me $149 for the couch?"
> B: "That is the fee for the pickup."
> C: "If possible I would like to schedule a pickup for this coming Monday."
*Demonstrates: one plain sentence resolving a confusion that killed several other conversations.*

**9. Fee-direction confusion, badly handled**
> C: "No worries! As in we pay $162 for pickup?"
> B: "Yes, that is our fee to pickup your sectional."
> C: "Oh! Didn't realize there was a fee. Nevermind I'll just donate to habitat for humanity!"
> [customer later returned and booked anyway]
*Demonstrates: how close this confusion runs to a lost deal. Preempt it.*

**10. Explaining the time window, then narrowing it**
> C: "That's too wide of a time frame. I work from home in sales. 10-11 or 3-5 would work."
> B: "Since we do between 7-10 pickups and deliveries every day, it's difficult to predict how long each one will take so we try to leave a minimum of a 2 to 3 hour timeframe for our drivers. If there's a specific 2 to 3 hour window that would work better for you, we can absolutely do our best to make that happen for you!"
> C: "Yes thats fine with me!"
*Demonstrates: constraint reframed as collaboration.*

**11. Not-home workaround**
> C: "Me and my husband are both teachers and can't be home until 3:30."
> B: "Are you able to bring them outside the house? We can pick up your couches even if you're not at home, as long as they are already placed outside and we can contact you by phone."
*Demonstrates: offering a solution instead of forcing a reschedule.*

**12. Curb discount closing a stalled deal**
> C: "We are on the third floor, and there is an elevator."
> B: "I can offer a discount and take $30 off the pickup fee for you if you bring it to the curb, which brings it down to $112. That's the best I can do."
> C: "We will bring it down to the curb. Wednesday should work."
*Demonstrates: trading customer effort for a fee reduction.*

**13. Drop-off as the cheaper alternative**
> C: "Would it be cheaper for us to drop it off?"
> B: "Yes. The total would only be $60 if you drop it off, versus $122 for pickup."
> C: "Ok I think your option will work best for us! Is pickup tomorrow still alright?"
*Demonstrates: presenting both options honestly; customer chose pickup anyway.*

**14. Explaining recliner surcharge, holding firm**
> C: "Wait why the surcharge? They are in good working condition with no rips or tears."
> B: "We usually have to charge for picking up recliners because of their weight and the difficulty of moving each piece. Our pickup cost covers a two-person crew, a box truck, labor, insurance, and the 23-mile drive. We feel it's a pretty competitive rate considering everything involved."
> C: "Ok I just send it via email."
*Demonstrates: itemized cost justification, no discount needed.*

**15. Incomplete sectional — customer challenges the logic**
> B: "We've had a bit of a tough time rehoming incomplete sectionals like this, so we're only able to offer pickup for a fee. Totally understand if you'd prefer to explore other options though, no worries at all!"
> C: "What alters it if it's complete or not?"
> B: "Thank you. We can offer to pick up the incomplete sectional for $246 since you're also 30 miles from our store. We can be there as soon as Monday, between 10–5 PM if that works for you."
*Demonstrates: a case where the agent quoted instead of answering the question. Better: explain that incomplete sectionals are much harder to resell than either a complete sectional or a standalone sofa, then quote.*

**16. Loyal customer questions the fee — full, honest explanation**
> C: "My couches were in good condition… no broken frames or mechanics, no rips, no pet damage, no major wear and tear, they were a solid color. Could you let me know how your pricing is determined? I'm happy to pay for your service, I believe in your cause. I'm just looking to understand how it works."
> B: "We do factor in 'used market value' into our fee, which isn't explained thoroughly on our website… We offer free pickups for couches that we know we can cover the cost of labor (and rent, insurance, etc) when we re-home them. Dark colors, heavy recliners, dated style, affected our ability to offer a free pickup for your couches."
*Demonstrates: the clearest explanation of the fee model in the dataset. Reuse this framing.*

**17. Returning customer, exception made**
> C: "Can you please explain the pricing? We are about 6 minutes away from you and purchased this couch for $400 from you guys in May of last year."
> B: "We usually have to charge to pickup recliners in general due to the weight and difficulty of moving each piece. I'm sorry, I know it really doesn't seem fair that we have to charge to pickup something that you bought from us. I can make an exception for you guys and give you 50% off our normal recliner pickup fee. ($70)"
*Demonstrates: naming the unfairness out loud, then acting on it. Booked.*

**18. Manager escalation turning a fee into free**
> C: "Oh okay - it's not a free donation?"
> B: "I just showed the photo to my boss and he offered free pickup."
> B: "I've got you down for Tuesday between 10-5 PM."
*Demonstrates: escalate borderline items rather than defending a quote you're unsure about.*

**19. Competing free offer — business matched it**
> C: "It's up to you—if you want it, you were first in line. If you'd rather not drive all the way up, I understand and will give it to him. I'm not going to take the $50 from him."
> B: "Oh, that's great! It's just difficult for us to make the trip up to [city] economical for us. The best I can do is to pick it up at no cost to you. If your neighbor can take it and you can make $50, that's a great deal! 🙂"
> C: "Hi--my neighbors did not take the sectional. Do you want to come pick it up Monday?"
*Demonstrates: honest, non-grasping response to competition — which won the item back.*

**20. Competing free offer — lost**
> B: "We can offer to pick up the couch, loveseat & ottoman for $219 since you're also 31 miles from our store."
> C: "After receiving your text that it would cost us $219, we posted it on Facebook Marketplace for free and within an hour someone came to pick it up. Thanks for your time."
*Demonstrates: free-alternative competition is real and often decisive. Nothing was mishandled here.*

**21. Hard price floor — walked**
> C: "I can do 50$"
> B: "I can offer a discount and take $30 off the pickup fee for you, which brings it down to $142. That's the best I can do."
> C: "I can do 50$"
*Demonstrates: when a customer repeats a number, further discounting won't bridge a $90 gap. Restate once, then let it go gracefully.*

**22. Distance too far — proactive redirect**
> B: "You're about 52 miles away, so the pickup cost would be pretty high. I'd recommend checking with an organization closer to you since they may be able to offer a lower pickup cost."
*Demonstrates: declining honestly builds goodwill. Several customers thanked the business for this.*

**23. Distance disclosed too late — customer frustrated**
> C: "I haven't heard from anybody please let me know ASAP if you'll be able to come out today… I have to either do it today or tomorrow so I need to figure this out with you guys."
> B: "Since you're 40+ miles from our store, the pickup cost would be pretty high. I'd recommend checking with an organization closer to you."
> C: "I wish you had told me that last week when I sent you the original address in [city]. Would've given me time to arrange for somebody else to pick up."
*Demonstrates: a legitimate complaint. Check distance and state it early.*

**24. Weekend request — clear no, immediate alternative**
> C: "Are you able to come on Saturday? Or after 3pm on Friday? If not, I can try to make Thursday work."
> B: "Sorry, we don't do pickups on Saturdays or Sundays. The only day we could do this week is Thursday since we're already fully booked on Friday. We can pick up the sectional for free and be there Thursday between 10-5pm."
> C: "Sounds good! Thursday works!"
*Demonstrates: firm constraint + concrete alternative in the same message.*

**25. Weekend request — no alternative offered, lost**
> C: "do you do Saturday pick ups?"
> B: "Sorry, we don't do pickups on Saturdays & Sundays"
> C: "Ok, well thanks anyway"
*Demonstrates: a bare "no" ends the conversation. Always pair with the nearest option.*

**26. Scope creep declined, deal preserved**
> C: "I also have a queen mattress, small computer table, and small vanity if you guys are able to remove that as well."
> B: "What floor are you on? Also, is there an elevator we can use? I'm sorry but we can only take the couch due to limited space."
*Demonstrates: decline the extras, keep momentum on the couch.*

**27. Scope creep declined, deal lost**
> C: "Before I send pics, can you please tell me if/how the other items will be picked up?"
> B: "We can only take the couches due to limited space."
> C: "I'll need to work with a company that can take everything. Have a nice day!"
*Demonstrates: correct answer, unavoidable outcome. Volunteering the couch-only scope in the first message would have saved time on both sides.*

**28. Donation receipt request — clear no**
> C: "I would like to know the cost to pick them up, and if you provide a tax donation receipt?"
> B: "We aren't a non-profit. We do work with many non-profits, including Coalition For The Homeless and a few sober living facilities. Unfortunately, due to our business model, we are unable to provide donation receipts."
*Demonstrates: complete, honest answer. Don't soften into ambiguity.*

**29. "Where does my couch actually go?" — trust question**
> C: "Thank you! And this would help the couch go to someone for free? We want to make sure it wouldn't go to the dump."
> B: "No problem! The couch would be brought to our warehouse, where it will be professionally cleaned, refurbished, and then rehomed."
> B: "Yes, this fee will allow us to cover the cost of refurbishment so we can rehome the couch without it ending up in the landfill. We do provide free furniture to less fortunate folks through organizations like Coalition for the Homeless and we also do have a shop where we sell some couches at low prices too."
*Demonstrates: environmental/charitable concern is a frequent motivator. Answer it substantively.*

**30. Same question, ignored — customer disengaged**
> C: "Will my couch and loveseat be giving for free to those in need???? Tell me about how my donation will help someone specifically please"
> B: "Hello, [name]! This is The Good Couch. I received your pickup request. Would you mind texting me photos of the couch & loveseat you need picked up, along with your address?"
*Demonstrates: sending the template over a real question. Answer first, then ask for photos.*

**31. Tip request feedback — customer pushback worth internalizing**
> C: "I will give them a tip, but if you send me a survey with feedback it will be negative. There should be a line that says 'free pickup', then next line should say 'if you think we did great a tip is always appreciated' vs a text the day of pickup asking for tips. By asking me you are 'obligating' me to tip."
> B: "The pickup is free! You are not obligated to tip."
*Demonstrates: a legitimate UX complaint, acknowledged. Frame tips as post-service and optional.*

**32. Refund/billing error handled well**
> C: "I accidentally did a tip of $200 instead of $25. He tried to do a refund for $175 but I see a charge on my cc transactions of $325.54 I just want to make sure the refund was properly done."
> B: "Hey [name]! Yes, I will send you a screenshot of the refund confirmation on my end. The refund typically takes 5-10 business days to go through."
*Demonstrates: proof + timeline. Both matter for billing concerns.*

**33. Repair request — clear scope boundary**
> C: "Hey my name is [name] and I bought a couch from yall a few years ago wondering about repairs? It's stuck in the recline position."
> B: "We dont offer repairs yet. We only repair if we are looking to re donate. Apologies!"
> C: "?"
*Demonstrates: the answer is right but too terse. The "?" signals the customer felt dropped. Add a referral or a suggestion.*

**34. Customer confusion about who they're talking to**
> C: "Or who is this. Leaving many messages. Is this Arc"
> B: "Hello, [name]! This is The Good Couch. Would you mind texting me photos of the couches you need picked up, along with your address? Once I have that, I'll be able to schedule your pickup. Thank you!"
*Demonstrates: re-identify by name immediately when a customer is unsure. Never skip the "This is The Good Couch" line.*

**35. Delayed response cost the job**
> C: "Hi! My address is [address], apt [unit]. Not sure if you can take the bookcase but let me know!"
> B: "Sorry for the very delayed response."
> C: "I sold the pieces already. Thanks."
*Demonstrates: speed is competitive. Dozens of conversations in the dataset ended with "someone already picked it up" or "I found someone on Marketplace."*

**36. Redundant request after info already provided**
> C: "[address] [2 photos attached]"
> B: "Hello, [name]! This is The Good Couch. I received your pickup request. Would you mind texting me photos of the couch you need picked up, along with your address?"
*Demonstrates: the most common self-inflicted error in the dataset. Read the thread before sending the template.*

**37. Scheduling error — confirmed a day the customer had ruled out**
> C: "Sorry but I'm busy all day Monday - I'm free Weds on"
> B: "Gotcha! I've got you down for Monday, between 10-5 PM."
*Demonstrates: a straightforward listening failure. Read back the day the customer actually gave.*

**38. Cancellation not acknowledged**
> C: "I need to cancel the pickup of the couch tomorrow. A neighbor asked if she could have it for their basement."
> B: "That works. I've got you scheduled for Wednesday between 10-5 PM."
*Demonstrates: confirming a booking after a cancellation. Always explicitly confirm removal from the schedule.*

**39. Contradictory offers within one conversation**
> B: "Actually, if you're able to do Friday, we could pick up your couch free of charge."
> C: "Thank you. ARC will do it for free. Have a great day."
> B: "Thank you. We can offer to pick up the couch for $90 and we can be there Friday or Tuesday next week between 10-5pm if that works for you."
*Demonstrates: free, then $90, in the same thread. Customer left. Never revise a quote upward without a stated reason.*

**40. Deferred close, handled patiently**
> C: "Okay. Let me discuss it with my husband. Is it okay if I let you know by tomorrow?"
> B: "Sure! Take your time"
*Demonstrates: no pressure. Roughly a hundred conversations involved a spouse/roommate/partner consult; pushing hurt more than it helped.*

**41. Timeline flexibility around a new-couch delivery**
> C: "I'm waiting to hear when the new couch will be delivered and we have company coming on Friday. Can I let you know as soon as I hear when the new couch will be delivered so we're not stranded without seating for our guests?"
> B: "Once you know when your new couch is being delivered, we'll work with you to schedule the pickup around that."
*Demonstrates: this is an extremely common constraint. Volunteering flexibility builds trust and often locks the job in.*

**42. Third-party pickup coordination**
> C: "I was coordinating this pickup for my neighbor. I will be at work if you get there in the morning, so I am sharing her number so that you can coordinate with her as she'll have to let you inside."
> B: "The drivers will reach out that morning with a more precise arrival time, and again when they're on the way."
*Demonstrates: when the requester isn't the on-site person, capture the on-site contact number explicitly. Ask for it if not offered.*

**43. Unrealistic sale-price expectation, handled with a helpful redirect**
> C: "this a $1200 couch. i need at least 500"
> B: "I'm sorry, but the pre-owned (perfect condition) market value is probably between $400-$500. If you need to sell it for that much, Facebook Marketplace is your best bet! We have to have some kind of margin in anything we buy, otherwise it doesnt make sense."
*Demonstrates: honest market reality + a genuinely useful alternative. No hard sell.*

## Common Pitfalls

**Requesting information the customer already sent.** By far the most frequent failure in this dataset — appears in 40+ conversations. The customer sends address and photos, then receives the standard "Would you mind texting me photos… along with your address?" template. This reads as inattentive and, in a few cases, visibly annoyed the customer ("The address is in the form. Do you need it again?"). Always read the full thread before sending any templated message.

**Not acknowledging cancellations.** Multiple conversations show a booking confirmation sent *after* the customer cancelled. One customer had to write: "Please let me know you got this, want to be sure that you don't send someone out on Monday." Confirm cancellations explicitly and immediately.

**Ignoring the customer's actual question and sending the intake template.** Customers asked about donation impact, insurance, whether the couch would go to a landfill, placement services, and repair — and got the photos-and-address script instead. Answer the question, then request what you need.

**Continuing the intake flow after the customer has said no.** Several transcripts show the agent asking for a pickup address after the customer said "I found another taker," "I'm going with Habitat for Humanity," or "we already disposed of these items." Acknowledge, thank them, leave the door open, stop.

**Revising a quote upward.** Free → $90. $80 → $150. $121 → $216. Discount to $111, then re-quote at $141. Customers asked "Can I ask why the $150 fee?" and did not always get an answer. If a quote must change, state the reason plainly and once.

**Confirming a day the customer already ruled out.** "I'm busy all day Monday" → "I've got you down for Monday." Same failure appeared with Tuesday/Wednesday and Thursday/Friday mix-ups. Read the day back.

**Quoting before checking access.** Repeatedly, a price was given and then the 3rd-floor-no-elevator, basement-with-turns, or storage-unit detail surfaced. Ask floor/elevator/stairs before quoting.

**Quoting before seeing photos.** Several conversations quoted a price, then requested photos, then had to reassess — including one where a customer sent a bad photo, got a quote, and was later told free pickup wasn't available after all. Photos first.

**Leaving the "am I paying you or are you paying me?" question ambiguous.** This confusion appeared dozens of times and cost at least several deals. Use the words "pickup fee" and say who pays whom.

**Answering fee questions with a non-answer.** One exchange: "What is the fee I didn't see it on the website" → "There is a pickup fee." That's not an answer. Give the number, or say you need photos and the address first to compute it — and say why.

**Terse rejections with no alternative.** "Sorry, we don't do pickups on Saturdays & Sundays." "We only take couches." "Nevermind." Every hard no should be paired with the nearest available option, a referral, or an explanation.

**Not stating distance limits early.** One customer was justifiably upset that they'd shared their address a week earlier and only learned about the 40+ mile problem when they were down to their last day. Check distance on receipt of the address.

**Slow response times.** "Someone just picked it up," "I went with another company," "ARC is coming tomorrow," "I sold it on Marketplace" — these appear in well over fifty conversations. This is a fast-moving, competitive market where free alternatives (Marketplace, Buy Nothing groups, ARC, Habitat ReStore, Goodwill, neighbors) are one message away.

**Front-loading tip requests.** A customer explicitly told the business that a day-of tip text after "free pickup" advertising felt obligating. Mention tips after service, framed as entirely optional.

**Asking the same qualifying question twice.** Several conversations show the pets/smoking or damage question asked after the customer already answered it. It reads as not listening. Track what's been answered.

**Offering a discount before the customer has objected.** In at least one case a $32 discount was offered, and only then did the customer reveal a $125 budget against a $222 quote. Let the objection surface first — you may not need to concede at all, and you'll know how far apart you actually are.

**Sending a message meant for another customer.** Happened multiple times. Recovered fine with a quick apology, but verify the thread before sending price quotes.

**Making promises for the drivers.** Don't commit the drivers to moving a mattress, breaking off legs, or helping with unrelated furniture. Say you'll pass it along or that they're welcome to ask on-site, and be clear it isn't guaranteed.

**Over-promising narrow arrival times.** The operational reality is a 2–3 hour minimum window with driver contact that morning. Don't commit to "we'll be there at 2:30."
